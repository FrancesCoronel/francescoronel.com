## Why I Did This

As a senior software engineer who's spent the last several years building product features in TypeScript and React, I've been increasingly surrounded by AI tooling without always understanding what's happening under the hood. I use Claude Code daily, I integrate LLMs into workflows, I talk about RAG and embeddings, but I wanted to actually *understand* the math and the mechanics, not just the API surface.

When I came across Deep Atlas, something clicked. One week. Full-time. SF. Cohort-based. Curriculum going from linear algebra all the way through LLM agents. That felt exactly like the focused, high-intensity experience I needed to level up.

And, maybe more importantly, I thought a few of my Slack colleagues would benefit just as much. So I rallied the troops.

---

## What is Deep Atlas?

[Deep Atlas](https://www.deepatlas.ai/) is an intensive AI/ML bootcamp that compresses roughly 6 months of learning into one week. Their immersive format runs Monday–Friday (9am–6:30pm) in San Francisco, with a cohort of ~25 engineers learning together.

The curriculum spans:

- **Math foundations**: linear algebra, calculus, statistics
- **Classical ML**: regression, classification, clustering
- **Deep learning**: neural networks, backprop, optimization
- **Pretrained models**: transfer learning, fine-tuning
- **Modern AI engineering**: RAG pipelines, agents, LLM evaluations

The through-line for me was finally connecting the dots on how LLMs actually work under the hood: transformer architecture, working through the "Attention Is All You Need" paper, RAG, agent orchestration, and MCP servers. I'd been using all of these concepts for months without fully understanding the mechanics beneath the API surface.

It's selective: you apply and interview before being accepted. 250+ alumni from the program work at companies like Google, Meta, and Stripe.

## The Week: Day by Day

### Day 1: Transformers and "Attention Is All You Need"

A very heavy lecture day, but I learned a lot, especially about the transformer architecture behind LLMs and the 2017 paper "Attention Is All You Need" that made what we use now possible. We also started our first project, a newsletter expert that uses RAG with an LLM.

### Day 2: RAG in Practice

I finished the newsletter expert project. Mine was **Substack in Slack**, which indexes 18+ of my Substack subscriptions and lets me ask questions about them from a Slack agent. Answers come back grounded in the actual posts with citations, streamed into a Block Kit UI, and retrieval is time-aware, so asking about "this week" actually means this week. I also volunteered to give the first lightning talk of the week! 🙋🏽‍♀️

### Day 3: Agent Orchestration

We talked about orchestration of agents being the new paradigm. And MCP everything, haha.

### Day 4: Project Time

This was mostly project time, so I kept thinking about what a UI could look like for an engineer managing a slew of agents at scale. That question became my final project.

### Day 5: Demo Day

Project presentations! I demoed Herd (more below 👇🏽), and we wrapped the week with a real group photo plus an AI-generated one that a classmate made from our pictures.

![The Deep Atlas MLI Cohort 20 group photo on Demo Day](/images/blog/deep-atlas/group-photo.jpg)

## My Lightning Talk

On Day 2, I gave the first lightning talk of the week, on how I turned Claude Code into my engineering co-pilot. I walked the cohort through my personal Claude Code setup and how I use AI in my day-to-day work as a senior engineer at Slack.

The core idea: build a system, not just use a tool. Before, every PR came with 15+ minutes of overhead (context gathering, writing descriptions, chasing conventions). Now that overhead is mostly handled by three layers:

- **CLAUDE.md is the brain**: the project context and conventions Claude reads every session
- **Skills are the muscle memory**: repeatable workflows I'd otherwise redo by hand
- **MCPs are the hands**: connections to the tools I actually work in

The response was genuinely overwhelming. Multiple people came up to me afterward and during the week to say it changed how they thought about integrating AI into their own work. A few shoutouts called it out directly (see below 👇).

## My Final Demo: Herd

Being the only frontend engineer in the program had me thinking about the UI/UX of how an engineer can orchestrate a slew of agents. So for demo day I built **Herd**, a dashboard that lets a shepherd (you) orchestrate AI coding agents working on your team's Jira backlog. Each ticket flows through a pipeline from backlog to merged PR, with the agent doing the coding and the human staying in control. 🐑

![Herd's About screen explaining the shepherd and agent workflow](/images/blog/deep-atlas/herd-about.png)

Click into any ticket and you can watch the agent work in real time, with the model, cost, tokens and cache hit rate right at the top:

![A Herd ticket's Chat tab showing an agent investigating a context menu bug](/images/blog/deep-atlas/herd-agent-chat.png)

The Timeline tab traces a ticket from creation to agent start, PR opened, review, approval and merge, and the Release tab follows it out through staging, canary and production:

![Herd's orchestration timeline for a completed ticket](/images/blog/deep-atlas/herd-timeline.png)

![Herd's deploy pipeline showing a release live in staging, canary and production](/images/blog/deep-atlas/herd-release.png)

After working on this proof of concept UI, I realized that having Slack as the surface layer for all of this would be much nicer, actually. 😅

## Key Takeaways

1. **The math isn't as scary as it looks.** Having Joe walk through gradient descent and backprop in a real classroom setting (where I could ask "dumb" questions without fear) made concepts click that I'd glossed over in blog posts for years.

2. **Cohort energy is a forcing function.** Working through projects alongside ~24 other engineers, some from companies I admire, pushed me harder than any self-paced course ever has.

3. **Claude Code is genuinely useful in an ML context.** My setup got multiple shoutouts from cohort-mates.

4. **Bringing colleagues makes it better.** Having a Slack crew in the room meant we could immediately talk about how to apply what we were learning to our actual work. That translation layer is huge.

5. **ML engineering and applying ML to product engineering are genuinely different disciplines.** I'd been conflating them for years. Understanding where they diverge (and how both connect to the agentic way of working with tools like Claude Code) was the single biggest unlock of the week. It's also what gave me the confidence to move from product engineering into DevXP. 🎉

## Would I Recommend It?

**Yes, with enthusiasm.** Deep Atlas is the real deal. Joseph Martin is one of the best instructors I've encountered: endlessly patient, genuinely passionate, and able to hold a room of engineers with varying backgrounds without losing anyone.

If you're a software engineer who wants to go from "I use LLMs" to "I understand what's actually happening inside them," this is the fastest, most human way to get there that I've found.

The immersive format is not for everyone. It's 9am–6:30pm for five days straight with a lot of material. But the cohort energy, the peer connections, and the compression of learning are genuinely worth it.

I was able to get the program fully expensed through my education budget. Worth checking if your employer offers something similar.

They run in-person cohorts in San Francisco and New York, plus a remote option. Check [deepatlas.ai](https://www.deepatlas.ai/) for upcoming dates.
